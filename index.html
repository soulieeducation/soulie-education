About Us

Growing together, beyond the classroom.

Children don't grow up alone. They grow inside families, classrooms, and communities. Soulie supports the whole circle.

Our mission

Soulie Education builds a resilient, empathetic, and connected learning ecosystem for children and families through social-emotional learning, parent education, accessible digital tools, and community-based learning experiences.

Our vision

A world where every child is supported in understanding themselves, navigating emotions, thinking critically, and building meaningful connections, and where families, educators, and communities grow together to make that possible.

Our belief

A child's development is shaped by the relationships and environments around them. When we support children alongside the adults who care for them, whole communities become more compassionate and resilient.

Soulie Education Inc. is a California nonprofit corporation. Our application for federal 501(c)(3) tax-exempt status is pending with the IRS.

Why Soulie exists

Education is about what children learn, and also who they become.

Academic skills matter. So does knowing what you feel, calming down when you're upset, and understanding someone else's point of view. These skills shape how children learn, play, and handle setbacks for the rest of their lives.

Yet many families and teachers don't have simple, practical tools for teaching them. Soulie turns child-development research and classroom experience into resources and experiences that parents, educators, and kids can use together.

Parent guide · SEL Talk Chips

Share. Listen. Grow.

How to use SEL Talk Chips at home, and how to make your own with AI so every question fits your child.

For parents & caregiversAges 4–1012 min read
Download printable (PDF)
What made you smile today? When do you feel brave? What if a friend says no? Who helped you this week? What calms you down? Pick a chip for me!
On this page What are Talk Chips? How to play at home Listening tips Make your own with AI Ready-to-use prompts Using AI safely Print & assemble

What are Talk Chips?

SEL Talk Chips are small round cards, each with one question on the front and a colored dot on the back. Kids pick a chip, read the question, and share. Everyone else listens. That's it.

The questions come in two kinds:

Reflection chipsLook back and notice feelings. "What was the hardest part of your day?"
Scenario chipsImagine and problem-solve. "Your friend won't share the swing. What could you do?"

Every chip has the same colorful-dot back, so nobody knows which question they'll get until they flip it over.

How to play at home

  1. Pick a calm moment. Dinner, bath time, the car ride, or bedtime all work. Five to ten minutes is plenty.
  2. Take turns drawing a chip. Put the chips in a jar or bag. Grown-ups play too, and your answers show kids it's safe to share.
  3. Share, then listen. One person talks at a time. Others listen without fixing or judging.
  4. "Pass" is always allowed. If a question feels too big, a child can pick a new chip. Feeling safe matters more than answering.
  5. End with something warm. Thank each other for sharing, or finish with an easy chip like "What are you looking forward to?"

Listening tips for grown-ups

  • Name the feeling back. "It sounds like you felt left out."
  • Get curious before giving advice. "What happened next?" "What did you wish would happen?"
  • Share your own small moments. "I felt nervous before my meeting today too."
  • Keep it light. Not every chip needs a lesson. Sometimes the win is simply talking together.
Try this tonight

Pick three chips: one easy, one reflection, one scenario. Notice which ones lead to the best conversations, and make more like those.

Make your own chips with AI

You know your child best. An AI assistant (like Claude or ChatGPT) can help you quickly write new chips around what your child loves, whether that's dinosaurs, soccer, a new sibling, or starting a new school.

  1. Choose a focus. A topic your child enjoys, or a skill you want to practice (sharing, frustration, being brave).
  2. Describe your child in general terms. Age and interests are enough. Leave out names and private details.
  3. Ask for a short list. Ten to fifteen questions at a time is easy to review.
  4. Read every question yourself. Keep the ones that sound like your family. Reword or delete the rest.
  5. Test and tweak. After a few rounds, ask the AI for "easier" or "more silly" versions of the chips your child liked.

Ready-to-use prompts

Copy a prompt, change the words in [brackets], and paste it into your AI assistant.

Make chips about an interest
Write 12 short conversation questions for a [6]-year-old who loves [dinosaurs]. Mix two kinds: 6 reflection questions about feelings and everyday experiences, and 6 scenario questions that ask "what would you do if…". Use simple words, keep each question under 12 words, and connect them to [dinosaurs] where it feels natural.
Practice a skill
My child is [7] and is working on [handling frustration when games don't go their way]. Write 10 gentle conversation questions that help them talk about this feeling and think of calm choices. Avoid blaming language. Include 2 questions I, the parent, can answer to model the skill.
Big change coming up
Our family is going through [a move to a new city]. Write 10 warm, hopeful conversation questions for a [5]-year-old about this change. Include questions about feelings, things they're excited about, and things they'll miss. Keep the tone safe and reassuring.
Make them easier or sillier
Here are some conversation questions my child enjoyed: [paste 3 questions]. Write 8 more in the same style, but [simpler for a younger sibling / a little sillier].

Using AI safely

Keep your child's privacy first
  • Don't type your child's full name, school, photos, or health details into an AI tool.
  • Describe needs in general terms ("a 6-year-old who gets nervous at drop-off").
  • AI can get things wrong. Always read questions before your child sees them.
  • Children shouldn't use AI tools on their own. This is a grown-up's job.

Print & assemble

  1. Download the free printable set below: 120 chips on 10 sheets (letter size), with a colorful-dot back for every page.
  2. Print double-sided on cardstock, flipping on the long edge, so each question lines up with a dot back.
  3. Cut out the 2.5-inch circles. A circle punch or paper cutter makes this quick.
  4. Store them in a jar, like the "Feelings" jar on our home page. Add your own AI-made chips any time.

Beyond Fidget Toys: Do They Really Help Kids Focus?

How sensory tools may support emotional regulation, with 4 everyday situations every parent can relate to.

BZBetty Zhang · Founder, Soulie EducationOctober 27, 202611 min read
On this page

Learning doesn't always mean sitting still

Growing up in China, I was used to a more traditional classroom: desks arranged in rows, students sitting quietly, and learning understood largely as listening and absorbing knowledge.

When I began teaching in private schools in the Bay Area, I encountered a different perspective. Learning didn't always mean sitting still. Sometimes, supporting a child meant offering movement, observing their needs, or finding a different way to help them engage.

During a math lesson, a classroom transition, or circle time, I've learned to look beyond a child's behavior and ask: What might they need in this moment?

You might see a child gently rocking on a cushion, another squeezing putty while working through a math problem, or a student quietly touching a textured strip on their desk while listening to a story.

These objects might look like ordinary fidget toys. But when used intentionally, they can serve another purpose: helping children manage sensory needs and participate in learning.

The question is, when does a fidget help, and when does it become just another distraction?

What are sensory regulation tools?

Sensory tools are objects or materials that provide sensory input through touch, movement, pressure, or other physical sensations. Examples include:

Tactile toolsTherapy putty, textured strips, and soft fabrics
Handheld fidgetsStress balls, fidget cubes, and quiet manipulatives
Movement supportsChair bands, wobble cushions, and movement breaks

The goal isn't simply to keep a child's hands busy. It's to find out whether a particular activity or object helps the child participate more comfortably.

A toy is usually designed primarily for play and entertainment. A sensory tool, when used in a learning environment, has a specific purpose.

For example, a pop-it might be a fun toy during free play. During a lesson, however, repeatedly popping every bubble could distract a child from listening. A quiet stress ball might be more appropriate if the goal is to provide something to squeeze while following instructions.

Tool or toy?

The difference isn't necessarily the object itself. It's how the child uses it and whether it supports the activity.

How can sensory tools help children focus?

Every child is different. Some children concentrate better when their hands are still; others seem more comfortable with a small amount of movement or tactile input.

Sensory tools may help some children manage restlessness, physical tension, or the discomfort of sitting through a demanding activity. However, research on fidget toys and classroom learning is mixed. They aren't proven to improve attention for every child, and some children become more distracted when using them.

Rather than assuming a fidget will work, start with two steps.

Step 1
Notice the need. Is the child becoming frustrated with a challenging task? Constantly moving their legs? Picking at classroom materials? Losing track of instructions? These observations can help you consider what kind of support might be useful. They don't automatically tell you why the behavior is happening.
Step 2
Try a strategy and observe. Offer an appropriate tool or alternative, explain how to use it, and watch what happens. Can the child return to the task? Are they following the discussion? Does the tool help them participate, or does it become the main attraction?

The goal isn't to eliminate every movement. It's to help the child engage with the activity.

4 everyday situations: when might a sensory tool help?

You don't need a classroom full of sensory equipment to support your child. Sometimes, the most useful place to start is by noticing what happens during everyday routines.

Here are four situations parents may recognize, along with strategies to try and signs to watch for.

These examples are illustrative, not clinical assessments. The suggested tools may help some children but won't work for everyone.

Case 1

Homework ends in tears

The struggle

Seven-year-old Leo sits down to work on his math homework. After a few difficult questions, he grips his pencil tightly, erases his answers repeatedly, and eventually bursts into tears.

What might be happening?

Leo may be frustrated by the difficulty of the work, worried about making mistakes, or struggling to manage his emotions. His clenched hands might also indicate physical tension.

Before reaching for a fidget, check whether he understands the assignment and whether he needs a break.

What you could try
  • Break the assignment into smaller, manageable steps.
  • Offer a short movement break or a quiet place to reset.
  • If Leo seems to benefit from squeezing something, try a soft stress ball during the next attempt.
What to observe
Does Leo return to the task more comfortably? Can he work through the next question? Or does he continue to feel overwhelmed?

Remember, a stress ball might help him manage tension, but it won't replace the support he needs to understand the math.

Case 2

Dinner time is a constant battle

The struggle

Six-year-old Maya struggles to remain seated during family dinner. She swings her legs, twists around in her chair, and repeatedly gets up. Her parents find themselves saying, "Sit down!" throughout the meal.

What might be happening?

Maya may need more movement, but other factors could be contributing. Is her chair comfortable? Are her feet supported? Is dinner lasting longer than she can comfortably manage?

What you could try
  • Make sure her feet have a stable place to rest, using a suitable footrest if needed.
  • Build movement into the evening before dinner.
  • Keep expectations appropriate for her age and the length of the meal.
  • If she enjoys tactile input, consider a quiet, safe object that won't interfere with eating.
What to observe
Does she become more comfortable when her feet are supported or after moving around? Can she participate in family conversation without constant reminders?

A fidget isn't necessarily the answer. Sometimes, adjusting the environment or routine makes a bigger difference.

Case 3

Story time doesn't hold their attention

The struggle

Five-year-old Ethan loves choosing bedtime books, but halfway through a story, he starts rolling around, touching everything nearby, or interrupting with unrelated questions.

His parent wonders whether a fidget would help him listen.

What might be happening?

Ethan may be restless, tired, eager to participate, or simply ready for a more interactive activity. Sitting through a long story can be demanding for some young children.

What you could try
  • Choose a shorter book or read in smaller sections.
  • Invite Ethan to turn the pages, point to pictures, or predict what happens next.
  • Let him change positions if he can still participate comfortably.
  • If he enjoys drawing, try letting him make a simple picture related to the story, provided it doesn't distract him from listening.
What to observe
Can Ethan tell you what happened in the story? Does he engage more when he can participate actively?

The goal isn't to keep his body perfectly still. It's to find a way for him to enjoy the story and engage with you.

Case 4

Fingers always need something to do

The struggle

Six-year-old Chloe constantly picks at clothing threads, peels labels off objects, shreds erasers, and scratches at furniture while watching television or doing homework.

Her parents are frustrated because she keeps damaging things, even after being reminded to stop.

What might be happening?

Chloe may enjoy tactile sensations, be acting out of habit, feel restless, or be looking for something to do with her hands. The behavior alone doesn't tell us which explanation is correct.

What you could try
  • Offer an appropriate alternative, such as a textured fabric square or a quiet, age-appropriate fidget.
  • Redirect her calmly when she starts damaging belongings.
  • Notice whether the behavior occurs mainly during waiting, listening, screen time, or stressful activities.
  • If picking causes skin damage or becomes difficult to interrupt, consult her pediatrician for guidance.
What to observe
Does Chloe use the alternative instead of picking at clothing or furniture? Does the behavior occur less often when she has something appropriate to do with her hands?

If the fidget becomes another distraction, try a different strategy rather than insisting she use it.

What do these situations have in common?

In each example, the behavior is only the starting point. The same behavior can have different causes, and the same tool can help one child while distracting another.

Before buying a fidget, try asking yourself three questions:

  1. When does this happen? Is it during homework, waiting, listening, or a particular transition?
  2. What have I already tried? Would a break, a change in the environment, clearer instructions, or more interaction help?
  3. What changes when I offer support? Does my child participate more comfortably, or does the difficulty remain?

You don't need to identify the exact sensory need on your own. Start by noticing patterns, trying reasonable adjustments, and discussing persistent concerns with your child's teacher or healthcare professional when appropriate.

Do all children need fidgets?

No. And children don't need an ADHD diagnosis to benefit from appropriate sensory or movement supports.

At the same time, fidgets aren't necessary for every child, and having one doesn't automatically make learning easier.

Sometimes, a child who struggles to sit through a lesson needs a movement break rather than a handheld object. A child who becomes frustrated during math might need clearer instructions. Another child might benefit from a quieter environment or more opportunities to participate.

It's also worth remembering that movement and fidgets aren't interchangeable. A child who needs to move their whole body may not find a small object satisfying, while a child who benefits from tactile input may not need to leave their seat.

A better question

The most useful question isn't, "Which fidget should I buy?" It's, "What is making this activity difficult for my child, and what support might help?"

Illustration via Unsplash

How can you tell if a fidget is actually helping?

If you're considering a sensory tool for your child, here are five things to look for.

  1. Your child can still participate. They can listen, follow instructions, answer questions, or complete the task.
  2. The tool doesn't take over. Your child isn't spending more time watching or manipulating the object than engaging with the activity.
  3. It addresses a specific need. You have a reason for trying the tool rather than offering it simply because it's popular.
  4. It works in the setting. The tool is age-appropriate, safe, and doesn't disrupt other children.
  5. You remain flexible. If it doesn't help, try another approach. A conversation with your child's teacher can help identify patterns across different activities and environments.

For young children, adults may initially need to help identify the problem and choose a strategy. Over time, children can practice recognizing when they need movement, a break, or another form of support.

If attention, sensory difficulties, or emotional challenges persist and interfere with everyday activities, consider discussing them with your child's pediatrician or an appropriately qualified occupational therapist.

The takeaway: the tool matters less than the need

Fidgets are popular for a reason: they're simple, accessible, and appealing to children. But a colorful object isn't automatically a learning tool.

As educators and parents, we can look beyond whether a child is sitting still and consider whether they're engaged, comfortable, and able to participate.

Sometimes the right support is a stress ball. Sometimes it's movement, clearer instructions, a change in the activity, or reassurance from a trusted adult.

The goal isn't to make every child sit perfectly still. It's to help each child find appropriate ways to participate, learn, and gradually understand what works for them.

And that starts with something simpler than buying another fidget: taking the time to observe the child in front of us.
